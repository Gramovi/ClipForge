A: Welcome back to The Deep End, the show where we talk about the stuff nobody teaches you. My guest today is Sarah, a former software engineer who quit a big tech job to build her own company and nearly lost everything in the process. Sarah, thanks for coming on.
B: Thanks for having me. And yes, nearly lost everything is an accurate description. I'm happy to talk about all of it.
A: Let's start with the moment you quit. You had a salary most people dream of. Why walk away?
B: Because I realized I was optimizing for the wrong thing. I was earning more every year, but every Sunday evening I felt sick thinking about Monday. And one day my manager told me I was on track to become a director in five years. I looked at the director and thought, I don't want his life. That was the moment.
A: A lot of people feel that, but very few actually leave. What made you different?
B: Honestly, I prepared for a year. People think quitting is one brave moment. It's not. I saved twelve months of expenses before I left. If you want one piece of advice from this episode, it's this: never quit your job without at least six months of runway. Courage is great, but runway is better.
A: Let's talk about negotiation, because you've said most people leave a lot of money on the table.
B: Almost everyone does. Here is the simplest trick. When they make you an offer, stay silent for five seconds. Just pause. Don't say yes immediately. Silence is uncomfortable, and the other person will often fill it by improving the offer. I've seen people get ten percent more just by not talking.
A: That sounds too simple to work.
B: It works because nobody does it. And the second rule is: never give the first number. If they ask what salary you expect, ask them what the budget for the role is. Whoever says a number first usually loses.
A: Now, you mentioned you nearly lost everything. What happened?
B: Our first product was an app for restaurants. We spent eight months building it without talking to a single restaurant owner. We were so sure we knew what they needed. When we finally launched, we called fifty restaurants. Forty nine said no. The one who said yes was my cousin.
A: Oh no.
B: Yes. Eight months and almost all of my savings gone. And the lesson was brutal but simple. Talk to customers before you write a single line of code. If you can't find ten people who will pay for your idea before it exists, it probably doesn't deserve to exist.
A: Let's switch to something people struggle with every day. Social media. How much time do you spend on your phone?
B: About forty minutes a day now. Two years ago it was over five hours. I deleted every social app from my phone and only check them on my laptop, once a day, at a fixed time. The first week felt like withdrawal. By the third week I was reading a book every week again.
A: Five hours to forty minutes. That's a huge change.
B: It is, and here's the thing nobody tells you. These apps are designed by thousands of very smart engineers whose only job is to keep you scrolling. It's not a fair fight. You can't win with willpower. You have to change the environment. Remove the app, and the habit disappears.
A: You also have a funny story about public speaking, right?
B: Oh, the conference. So I was invited to speak in front of about two thousand people. I was terrified. Right before I went on stage, I drank a big glass of water to calm down. Halfway through the talk, I realized my microphone was still on from when I had gone to the bathroom backstage. The sound engineer came up to me afterwards and said, everyone heard everything.
A: No way.
B: Everything. And you know what? It was the most popular talk of the whole conference. People came up to me for days. The lesson is that nobody remembers perfect. They remember human. Stop trying to be flawless and start trying to be real.
A: Let's talk about learning. You speak four languages. How?
B: I learned the last two as an adult, and the secret is embarrassingly simple. Fifteen minutes every single day beats three hours once a week. Your brain needs repetition and sleep in between. I used to cram on weekends and forget everything by Wednesday. Small daily sessions changed everything.
A: What about people who say they are too old to learn a language?
B: That's a myth. Adults actually learn grammar and vocabulary faster than children. Children just have more time and less fear of sounding stupid. If you can let go of the fear of sounding stupid, you can learn anything at any age.
A: Money question. What would you tell someone in their twenties about investing?
B: Start now, even if it's tiny. If you invest two hundred dollars a month from age twenty five with an average return of seven percent, you'll have around five hundred thousand dollars at sixty five. If you wait until thirty five, you'll have about half of that. Ten years of waiting costs you a quarter of a million dollars. Time is the most valuable thing you have in investing.
A: That's a sobering number.
B: It's the most important math most people never do. And please, buy boring index funds. The exciting stuff your friends talk about at parties is how people lose money.
A: Do you have a morning routine?
B: I do, but it's not one of those crazy four a.m. routines. I wake up at seven, I drink a glass of water, I go outside for ten minutes of daylight, and I write down the one thing that would make the day a success. Just one. Most people write to do lists with twenty items, finish five, and feel like failures. One thing done is a win.
A: That's refreshingly realistic.
B: The best routine is the one you can do on your worst day. If it only works on good days, it's not a routine, it's a hobby.
A: Last topic. Fear of failure. You've failed publicly. How do you deal with it now?
B: I keep a failure resume. It's a document listing every rejection, every failed project, every mistake. It's longer than my real resume. And whenever I'm scared to try something new, I read it and remember that I survived all of those. Failure is not the opposite of success. It's part of it.
A: Sarah, if you could tell your twenty year old self one thing, what would it be?
B: Nobody is thinking about you as much as you think they are. Everyone is busy worrying about themselves. So go ahead and do the embarrassing thing. Ask the question. Apply for the job. Start the company. The worst case is usually much smaller than your fear tells you.

B: Thank you, this was really fun.
