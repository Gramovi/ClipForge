A: We're back for part two with Sarah. Sarah, before the break you mentioned hiring. What's the biggest hiring mistake founders make?
B: Hiring people exactly like themselves. When I started, I hired three engineers who thought just like me. We agreed on everything, and that was the problem. Nobody told me when I was wrong. Now I deliberately hire people who will argue with me. Disagreement is cheap. Building the wrong thing is expensive.
A: How do you test for that in an interview?
B: I describe a decision I made recently and ask them to tell me what's wrong with it. If they only say nice things, that's a red flag. I want someone who can say, here's the risk you didn't see, respectfully but clearly.
A: Let's talk about burnout. You mentioned you hit a wall in your second year.
B: I did. I was working eighty hours a week and I thought that was what commitment looked like. Then one morning I couldn't get out of bed. Not because I was sick, but because I simply couldn't face another day. It took me four months to feel normal again.
A: What would you do differently?
B: I would treat rest as part of the work, not a reward for finishing it. Your brain is the tool you use to build the company. You wouldn't run a machine at full speed for a year without maintenance. And yet that's exactly what we do to ourselves.
A: Do you have practical rules now?
B: Three. No email after eight p.m. One full day off every week, no exceptions. And a walk without my phone every afternoon. The walk is where I get my best ideas. Every single important decision this year started on one of those walks.
A: Let's do a quick money topic. Credit cards. Good or bad?
B: A credit card is a tool, like a knife. In the right hands it's useful, in the wrong hands it hurts you. If you pay the full balance every month, you get points and protection for free. If you carry a balance, you're paying twenty percent interest, and no investment in the world reliably beats that. Paying off a credit card is the best guaranteed return you will ever get.
A: That's a great way to put it.
B: And here's a trick for big purchases. Wait thirty days. Write down what you want and the date. If you still want it after thirty days, buy it. Most of the time you'll forget you ever wanted it.
A: What about side projects? Everyone seems to have one now.
B: I love side projects, but most people start too many. Pick one and give it ninety days. Not ninety days of planning, ninety days of shipping. Put something in front of real people every week. After three months, you'll know whether it deserves more of your life.
A: You've talked a lot about failure. What's a success you're proud of?
B: Honestly, the thing I'm proudest of isn't the company. It's that two people who started as interns with me now run their own businesses. Building people lasts longer than building products.
A: Final question. What's one book everyone should read?
B: Any book about a field you know nothing about. Reading only about your own industry makes you narrow. The best ideas come from connecting things that don't seem related. I learned more about product design from a book about architecture than from any startup book.
A: Sarah, thank you, this has been fantastic.
B: Thank you. And to everyone listening, start before you feel ready. You never will.
