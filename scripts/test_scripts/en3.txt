A: Sarah, we have a few listener questions before we wrap up. The first one is from a nurse who wants to switch careers into tech at forty two. Is it too late?
B: Not at all, and I'd argue she has an advantage. A nurse understands pressure, people and systems that can't fail. Health tech companies desperately need people who know how hospitals really work. My advice is don't start from zero. Start from where you are. Look for the intersection between what you already know and the new field. That's where you're rare, and rare is valuable.
A: Practically, where should she begin?
B: Pick one small project that solves a problem she sees at work every day. Maybe a better shift schedule spreadsheet, maybe a simple app for medication reminders. Build it badly, show it to colleagues, improve it. A real project beats ten online certificates on a resume.
A: Second question. Someone asks how to deal with a boss who takes credit for their work.
B: Oh, that's painful, and very common. First, make your work visible in writing. Send a short weekly summary email to your boss and copy the relevant people. Not as an attack, just as an update. What I finished, what I'm working on, what's blocked. It creates a record, and it's very hard to steal credit from something already documented.
A: And if that doesn't work?
B: Then have a direct, calm conversation. Say, I'd love to present this project to the leadership team myself, it would help my growth. Most people who take credit do it because nobody pushes back. And if nothing changes after that, it's time to look for a new boss. You can't out-work a bad manager.
A: Third question, and this one is about money again. Should I pay off my student loans first or invest?
B: Look at the interest rate. If your loan charges more than about six or seven percent, pay it off aggressively, because that's a guaranteed return. If it's below four percent, investing usually wins over the long run. And in between, do both. Split the extra money in half. Personal finance is personal, but the math is not complicated.
A: People love a clear rule.
B: And here's another one. Always take the free money first. If your employer matches retirement contributions, contribute at least enough to get the full match before anything else. It's an instant hundred percent return. Nothing else on earth gives you that.
A: Let's do one about productivity. A listener says she has two hundred unread emails every morning and feels like she's drowning.
B: I used to be her. Here's what fixed it for me. I check email only three times a day, at ten, at one and at four. Outside those times the app is closed. And I use a rule for every email: if it takes less than two minutes, I do it right now. If it takes longer, it goes on my task list with a date. If it's not important, I archive it without guilt.
A: Without guilt is the hard part.
B: It is. But here's the secret. Most emails are other people's priorities pretending to be yours. If something is truly urgent, people will call you. In five years of checking email three times a day, I have never missed anything that actually mattered.
A: Next question is more personal. How do you stay motivated when nothing seems to be working?
B: I track inputs, not outcomes. Outcomes are partly luck. If I'm trying to get customers, I can't control how many say yes, but I can control how many I talk to. So my goal isn't ten new customers this week, it's fifty conversations. When you focus on inputs, you feel progress every single day, even when results are slow. And results always lag behind effort.
A: That's a powerful reframe.
B: It changed my whole relationship with failure. A no is no longer a failure. It's one of the fifty conversations I planned. It's just data.
A: Last listener question. What's the most underrated skill for the next ten years?
B: Writing clearly. Not beautiful writing, clear writing. As more work becomes remote and more of it involves AI tools, the people who can explain exactly what they want, in plain words, will have a huge advantage. Clear writing is clear thinking. If you can't explain your idea in one paragraph, you don't understand it yet.

B: Write one paragraph every day explaining something you learned. Then cut it in half. Then cut it in half again. It's painful, but after a month you'll notice that people actually read your emails and do what you asked.
